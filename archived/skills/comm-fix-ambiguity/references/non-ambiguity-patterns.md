
# Non-ambiguity

Each detectable form of ambiguity is an individual pattern with an ID, a question to ask, the condition under which it is a problem, the condition under which it is not, the patterns it is most often confused with, and an example. Each pattern carries a taxonomy tag, N1 to N6, naming the kind of missing meaning it detects. The tags are defined once, in the taxonomy section, and are labels rather than groups.

## What ambiguity is

Ambiguity is unresolved meaning in a communication: the recipient can reasonably interpret it in materially different ways, or cannot determine what an important part refers to or means. It can concern an object, an action, a purpose, a criterion or the relationship between otherwise clear statements.

The writer may know exactly what they mean while leaving the recipient without that understanding. Correct grammar and familiar words do not ensure that the necessary meaning has been communicated. Naming the right objects can still leave their actions or connections unexplained.

Whether ambiguity is a problem depends on the recipient's task and the context actually available to them. Ask which distinctions they need to understand this communication, and whether they can establish those distinctions without guessing.

## Why ambiguity creates friction

- **It transfers explanatory work to the recipient.** They must reconstruct previous discussions, search other material or ask follow-up questions before they can understand the point.
- **It can produce silent misunderstanding.** A recipient may confidently select a plausible meaning that differs from the author's intent, then judge the wrong proposal, act on the wrong object or assume a commitment that was never made.
- **It disrupts coordination and continuity.** People or AIs can appear to agree while referring to different things. Later work inherits that mismatch, causing avoidable rework and making the communication harder to rely on.

The consequence matters more than the presence of a vague word. An ambiguity warrants a finding when it obstructs or changes understanding needed for the exchange.

## When ambiguity is acceptable or necessary

- **Exploration and emerging ideas:** A concept may deliberately have provisional boundaries or several possible interpretations. Forcing a final definition or decision can close down the exploration the communication is meant to support.
- **Unresolved knowledge or choices:** The cause of a problem, an estimate or a decision may genuinely be unknown. Preserve that uncertainty; filling it with a plausible answer would misrepresent the situation. Uncertainty can itself be communicated clearly.
- **Narrative and expressive purposes:** Suspense, metaphor, humour or an invitation to interpret can depend on leaving meaning open. Removing every possible interpretation can defeat the intended effect.
- **Detail outside the present purpose:** Some distinctions can remain unspecified because the recipient does not need them for this exchange. Precision should serve understanding rather than force irrelevant detail into the message.

Established shared context can also make shorthand clear: “these workflows” is sufficient when the relevant workflows have already been identified. Judge the whole delivery, including context the recipient legitimately has, rather than requiring every sentence to stand alone.

Preserving openness does not exempt the rest of the text from review. The subject, known facts and relevant connections should remain understandable. Make clear what is unresolved when the recipient needs that distinction, without demanding that it be resolved.

## How to use the patterns

### Patterns are prompts, not gates

**The patterns are not an exhaustive catalogue. Actively identify unlisted forms.** Different words, subjects, media or arrangements of information can produce the same underlying failure. A passage does not need to resemble a pattern's wording, example or situation to warrant a finding. Each taxonomy tag has an “Other” pattern, P05, P09, P15, P21, P26 and P32, for a supported gap that matches the tag's question but none of the listed forms; describe the missing meaning when you use it.

### Read from the recipient's position

Read each paragraph with its heading and surrounding explanation. Establish what it needs to convey and what context the recipient actually has at that point. Ask: “Without the author's private working context, what would I understand here?” Use established shared context; do not assume either complete ignorance or knowledge the recipient has not received.

### Examine every sentence in order

**Work sentence by sentence within each paragraph. Complete each sentence's examination before moving to the next.** Inspect its exact content, including small details that change meaning:

- Which objects do its names, nouns and pronouns identify?
- Who or what acts, what happens, and what does the action affect?
- What purpose, criterion, condition or relationship does the sentence rely on?
- Do words such as “only”, “still”, “then” or “may” change scope, sequence or certainty?

Then go down the pattern index in order, P01 to P32, and ask each pattern's question wherever it could apply. The taxonomy tag on a pattern is a label for the kind of missing meaning; it does not group patterns and does not let one stand in for another. A sentence can match several patterns, and one pattern can apply to several expressions in the same sentence. Finding one gap does not finish the sentence. Determine which answers the reader needs and where the communication supplies them. Check overlapping concerns together; fixing one may leave another unresolved. Examine short sentences, headings, bullets and table entries too, using their labels and context.

### Check how the sentences fit together

After examining the sentences, reread the paragraph as a whole. Check its purpose, order, transitions and connections, including necessary explanations absent from every sentence. Then check relationships across paragraphs and the whole communication; the connection patterns, P27 to P32, apply at this level as well as within a passage. Locally clear sentences can still form an unclear account.

### Keep context across chunks

When reviewing chunks, use the invoking workflow's assigned ranges and surrounding context. If a boundary splits a sentence or paragraph, read the complete unit while retaining its original location. Follow necessary references beyond the neighboring excerpts within the permitted sources. A chunk boundary is not a defect or a limit on meaning; reconcile findings across overlaps without counting the same problem twice.

### Report supported problems

Identify the specific meaning the recipient needs but cannot establish, and its consequence. Label each finding with the pattern ID, a short statement of the missing meaning, and the category tag, for example `P20 — condition for escalation unstated: “if still failing” [N4]`. When a passage has several gaps, list each one. Two findings under the same pattern on one passage are two gaps only when they concern different affected expressions or different missing meanings; otherwise they are one. When one missing meaning recurs across many passages because the document never supplies it, that is one finding: report it once, listing every affected location, rather than once per passage. Preserve intended openness and legitimate shared context. Report findings without printing clean-sentence assessments. Examples illustrate judgments, not current facts; propose clarification from available evidence without inventing missing details.

## Ambiguity taxonomy

Every pattern carries one of six tags. A tag names the kind of missing meaning, states the question behind it and the limits that apply to every pattern carrying it. Tags are labels for reporting and for the limits; they are not groups. Do not treat patterns with the same tag as one check, and do not let a match under one pattern cover another with the same tag.

### N1 — Identifiable referents

- **Question:** For each important name, noun, pronoun or shorthand, which thing or set does it mean, and where can the recipient identify it?
- **Limits:** Use the text and legitimate shared context. Established names and pronouns need no repeated definition. Preserve ambiguity deliberately serving the brief; do not guess an unknown referent.
- **Why it matters:** The reader must reconstruct private context and may understand a different object, task or decision—or apply the right action to the wrong thing.

### N2 — Object roles and relationships

- **Question:** What does each important object represent, what does it contribute, and how does it connect to the others?
- **Limits:** Require only relationships needed for this explanation. Do not demand identifiers, locations or system descriptions when the connection is already clear, or invent a relationship unsupported by the sources.
- **Why it matters:** The reader may recognise every name yet form an incorrect account of the work—for example, confusing a stored description with the system it describes or misreading which objects are accessible.

### N3 — Concrete actions and actual actors

- **Question:** For each consequential action, who or what acts, what operation occurs, what does it act on, and what output or change results?
- **Limits:** Actors may be people, teams, applications or workflows. Passive wording and descriptive sentences are acceptable when clear. Naming an actor does not establish a permanent owner or require assigning one.
- **Why it matters:** The reader cannot tell what actually happens, may attribute work to the wrong person or system, or may inspect a different output from the one intended.

### N4 — Purpose, scope and judgment criteria

- **Question:** For which purpose? Compared with what? Within which scope? By which criterion?
- **Limits:** Qualitative criteria can be sufficient. Do not invent numerical thresholds or finalise criteria meant to remain open. An exploratory question may name the unresolved criterion without answering it.
- **Why it matters:** The reader may answer a different question, accept a broader claim or judge the work against a standard the author did not intend while believing the meaning is shared.
- **Caution:** These questions can be asked of almost any sentence. Report a gap only when the answer is needed for this communication and cannot be established from the text or legitimate shared context.

### N5 — Prerequisite context available to the reader

- **Question:** What must the recipient understand before this point makes sense, and where and when do they receive it?
- **Limits:** A standalone document and a reply in an established thread have different context needs. Use legitimate shared context and explanations available in the intended delivery; include necessary background rather than the entire history. Do not assume every recipient shares the operator's context or recall needs. Qualify uncertainty about reader knowledge or uninspected supporting material. Preserve intentional withholding and open choices that serve the task.
- **Why it matters:** The reader must reconstruct the situation or its relevance and may misunderstand what an example, instruction or conclusion is meant to establish.

### N6 — Connected sections and compatible claims

- **Question:** What question does each section answer, what role does each point play, and do the parts form one compatible, understandable account, including across delivery surfaces?
- **Limits:** Different viewpoints, time periods and levels can coexist when their relationship is clear. A heading need not be a question. Preserve intentional narrative structure and unresolved alternatives; assess evidential support separately under Meaning and fidelity.
- **Why it matters:** Individually clear parts can produce an incoherent account, hide a dependency or lead the reader to a conclusion about a different question from the one the communication set out to answer.

## Consequence groups

Every pattern belongs to one of two groups, fixed here and never judged at detection time.

- **action** — the missing meaning concerns what is done: the operation, its actor, its target or extent, its condition, the addressee of an instruction, or the scope of a rule. Patterns P10 to P15, P18 and P20.
- **meaning** — the missing meaning concerns what is being conveyed: a referent, a relationship, a criterion, a prerequisite or a connection between parts. All other patterns.

The group combines with one property of the affected sentence, recorded by the detector as `kind:` and checkable against the quoted words: an **instruction** tells its reader to do or not do something, or states a rule the reader must follow; a **statement** describes, explains or names. The consequence rating is then a lookup from the table below, transcribed by the detector once the kind is recorded, never judged:

| | action pattern | meaning pattern |
|---|---|---|
| instruction sentence | vital: the reader could do the wrong thing | high: the reader must ask or look up before acting |
| statement sentence | high: the described action is unclear | medium: understanding is reduced; nothing is done wrongly |

The rating is a triage aid, not a truth about the finding: a statement can be the premise of an action elsewhere and will still rate medium. How many independent readings reported a finding remains the signal of how far to trust it.

## Patterns

Thirty-two patterns in ID order. Examine each one on its own. The taxonomy line names the kind of missing meaning a pattern detects and points to the limits that apply to it; it is a label, not a group, and no pattern stands in for another.

### P01 — A generic name, number or set without the subject that identifies it

- **Taxonomy:** N1 · Identifiable referents
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “A plan for what? Which five? Which checks?”
- **Problem when:** A generic noun, count or set is used where the intended reader follows several candidates, and neither the text nor legitimate shared context picks out which one. The gap can remain after a generic noun has been replaced with a more specific one.
- **Not a problem when:** The preceding text or the delivery context has already established the subject, or only one candidate is plausible for this recipient.
- **Distinguish from:** P03 when the subject is clear but its place or version is not; P04 when the name is specific but has never been introduced.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** The author is reporting work on evaluating AI models for moderation. The recipient follows several projects with test plans, and this standalone update has not identified its subject.
  - **Bad:** “The next step is writing the test plan.”
  - **Good:** “The next step is writing the test plan for comparing AI models on moderation tasks.”
  - **What changes:** The reader can identify which plan is meant. Ask “a plan for what?” Repeating the full subject would be unnecessary if the preceding text already established it.

### P02 — A pronoun or shorthand with more than one plausible source

- **Taxonomy:** N1 · Identifiable referents
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Its, this, these, that one: which of the candidates in play?”
- **Problem when:** Two or more referents are plausible for a pronoun, demonstrative or shorthand, and choosing differently would materially change the point.
- **Not a problem when:** Only one source is plausible once the surrounding text is read. The pronoun itself is not the defect.
- **Distinguish from:** P01 when the expression is a generic noun rather than a back-reference; P08 when both referents are clear but their relationship is not.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A paragraph discusses a sample preparation framework and questions sent to a colleague.
  - **Bad:** “Do its questions help us identify test cases?”
  - **Good:** “Do the sample preparation framework's questions help us identify test cases?”
  - **What changes:** The question set is identified. The problem is the competing referent, not the use of a pronoun itself; “its questions” can work once only one source is plausible.

### P03 — An object named without the place, table or version that distinguishes it

- **Taxonomy:** N1 · Identifiable referents
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Which record, in which table? Which draft, which version, which environment?”
- **Problem when:** Several locations, systems or versions hold objects of that kind, the reader needs the distinction, and the text does not supply it.
- **Not a problem when:** Only one location or version is in play, or the distinction does not matter for this exchange.
- **Distinguish from:** P01 when the object's subject itself is unclear; P06 when the confusion is between a record and the thing it records.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** The author means a record in Airtable's Workflows table. The reader knows which workflow is being discussed, but several tables contain records about workflows.
  - **Bad:** “The workflow has no row of its own.”
  - **Good:** “The workflow has no record describing it in Airtable's Workflows table.”
  - **What changes:** “Row” now has an identifiable table and subject. Naming the workflow alone did not identify the missing record. Location matters here because it distinguishes the intended object.

### P04 — A name used at first mention without introduction

- **Taxonomy:** N1 · Identifiable referents
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Has this recipient met this name, here or in legitimate shared context?”
- **Problem when:** A proper name, label, feature, place or team is used as if known, and the intended recipient has no way to identify what it is or where it lives at that point.
- **Not a problem when:** The recipient legitimately knows the name, the same passage introduces it, or a visible surface identifies it at that moment.
- **Distinguish from:** P23 when an introduction exists but arrives later; P24 when it exists only in private material; P01 when the name is generic rather than specific.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A team-chat message to a colleague who joined this week. The project board has a column called Parking Lot, which the message treats as known.
  - **Bad:** “Move the two Slack items to the Parking Lot before Thursday.”
  - **Good:** “Move the two Slack items to the Parking Lot, the board column for ideas we are not scheduling yet, before Thursday.”
  - **What changes:** The name is introduced at the point of first use. For a colleague who already works on the board, the original is sufficient; the pattern depends on who receives the message.

### P05 — Other unidentifiable referent

- **Taxonomy:** N1 · Identifiable referents
- **Group:** meaning
- **Use when:** A supported gap matches N1's question but none of P01–P04. State the expression and the missing distinction in the label.

### P06 — A record or description confused with the thing it describes

- **Taxonomy:** N2 · Object roles and relationships
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Is this the thing, or the entry about the thing?”
- **Problem when:** The text treats a record, entry, page or description and the system, work or object it describes as one thing, and the reader needs to know which is being created, checked or changed.
- **Not a problem when:** Only one of the two exists in the situation, or the distinction changes nothing for this exchange.
- **Distinguish from:** P03 when both are distinct but the record's location is missing; P08 when the two things are distinct but their relationship is unstated.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** An n8n workflow already exists; the proposed work is adding its descriptive record to Airtable.
  - **Bad:** “We will create the missing workflow in Airtable.”
  - **Good:** “We will add an Airtable Workflows record describing the existing n8n workflow. The workflow itself runs in n8n.”
  - **What changes:** The executable workflow and its descriptive record have distinct roles. Knowing both product names does not tell the reader which object is being created.

### P07 — A method, tool or framework named without how it applies to the material

- **Taxonomy:** N2 · Object roles and relationships
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “What does each thing contribute, and what is done with it?”
- **Problem when:** A method and its material are both named, but the reader cannot tell what the method supplies, what the material supplies, or how they are used together.
- **Not a problem when:** The application is conventional for this recipient, or the sentence only needs to name the ingredients and the application follows in the same passage.
- **Distinguish from:** P11 when the problem is a vague verb rather than a missing role; P08 when neither object is a method.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** The reader knows the sample preparation framework and the selected comment records, but not how they will be used together.
  - **Bad:** “We will check the sample preparation framework against the records.”
  - **Good:** “We will use the framework's questions to identify the context needed for each selected comment, then check whether those questions help us find that context in the records.”
  - **What changes:** The framework supplies questions; the records provide material on which to try them. Ask what each object contributes to the comparison, not merely whether both are named.

### P08 — The relationship between two named things left to reconstruction

- **Taxonomy:** N2 · Object roles and relationships
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Which one describes, uses, contains, produces, constrains or overlaps the other? Are these two things, one thing, or parts of one thing? What is this evidence evidence of?”
- **Problem when:** Both ends are identifiable, but the reader must guess the direction, overlap or distinction between them, and guessing differently changes the account. Includes two named tools, products or components whose difference matters and is never stated, and evidence whose subject is unstated.
- **Not a problem when:** The relationship is already established, is conventional for the recipient, or is not needed for this explanation.
- **Distinguish from:** P02 when one end is itself unidentified; P06 when the relationship is record versus thing recorded; P31 when the missing link is a dependency between sections rather than between objects.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** An Airtable token allows access to 15 bases. The workflows being inspected write results into 10 bases, only one of which is accessible with that token.
  - **Bad:** “The token reaches 15 bases, these workflows write into 10, and nine are outside it.”
  - **Good:** “Our Airtable token lets us read 15 bases. Of the 10 bases receiving these workflows' results, only one is accessible with that token; we cannot read the other nine.”
  - **What changes:** The overlap between the permission set and the destination set is explicit. The reader can understand the access limitation without guessing what “outside it” describes.

### P09 — Other unclear role or relationship

- **Taxonomy:** N2 · Object roles and relationships
- **Group:** meaning
- **Use when:** A supported gap matches N2's question but none of P06–P08. State the objects and the missing relationship in the label.

### P10 — Objects or a case named, but the action left unstated

- **Taxonomy:** N3 · Concrete actions and actual actors
- **Group:** action
- **Ask:** “What will be done with these? And if that case arises, then what?”
- **Problem when:** A sentence names the objects of an action, or raises a condition or case, without saying what operation happens to them or what to do when the case holds.
- **Not a problem when:** The action is established by the preceding text, or the sentence is intentionally a list of objects for an action already described.
- **Distinguish from:** P11 when an action is named but too broad to understand; P20 when the case is named and handled but the test for it is unclear.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** The proposed action is enabling execution-log saving for selected workflows.
  - **Bad:** “The five workflows I'd start with.”
  - **Good:** “The five workflows for which I'd enable execution-log saving first.”
  - **What changes:** The reader knows what will be started. Changing “the five” to “the five workflows” fixes object identity but still leaves the action unspecified.
- **Also, the raised-case form:**
  - **Bad:** “Check which printer is selected. If there are several, note the default.”
  - **Good:** “Check which printer is selected. If there are several and the default is not the one in your office, select yours before printing.”
  - **What changes:** The raised case now has its handling. Noticing a condition is not an instruction for what to do about it.

### P11 — A broad verb or action noun hides the operation and its output

- **Taxonomy:** N3 · Concrete actions and actual actors
- **Group:** action
- **Ask:** “Concretely, what happens, and what exists or changes afterwards?”
- **Problem when:** Verbs such as handle, process, check, verify, sort out, or nouns such as verification, follow-through, review, stand for an operation the reader cannot reconstruct, or leave the resulting output or state undetermined when the reader needs it.
- **Not a problem when:** The operation is conventional for this recipient, or the sentence deliberately summarises work described concretely elsewhere in the delivery.
- **Distinguish from:** P10 when no action is named at all; P13 when the operation is clear but its target or extent is not; P16 when the operation is clear and only the standard for judging its result is missing.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** The question concerns the “HP Moderation Questions” and “Explore Disney+ Cross-linking Suggestions” workflows. In this illustration, both send results to Airtable, and neither has been identified in the preceding text.
  - **Bad:** “Are these workflows still writing anything?”
  - **Good:** “Have the HP Moderation Questions and Explore Disney+ Cross-linking Suggestions workflows added new result records to their destination Airtable tables?”
  - **What changes:** Two gaps are addressed: the names identify which workflows, and “added new result records” identifies the operation and output being checked. Fixing “writing anything” alone would leave the subject ambiguous. If preceding text already identifies these workflows, “these workflows” is sufficient; otherwise use their specific names or an equally identifiable set description.

### P12 — The actor unstated, hidden or misattributed

- **Taxonomy:** N3 · Concrete actions and actual actors
- **Group:** action
- **Ask:** “Who or what does this? Would naming a different actor change the account?”
- **Problem when:** A consequential action has no identifiable actor, is phrased so that an automated actor reads as a person or the reverse, or is attributed to an actor who does not perform it. Includes “needs approval” or “is verified” with no one to approve or verify.
- **Not a problem when:** The passive is clear from context, or the actor is deliberately unassigned and the text says so.
- **Distinguish from:** P14 when the uncertainty is which of two audiences an instruction addresses; P11 when the actor is clear and the operation is not.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** An automated synchronisation workflow copies each n8n workflow's enabled state into its Airtable record.
  - **Bad:** “Whether a workflow is enabled comes from n8n, automatically.”
  - **Good:** “The synchronisation workflow reads whether each workflow is enabled in n8n and copies that value into its Airtable record.”
  - **What changes:** The source, operation and destination become understandable. The actor is an automated workflow; replacing it with a person would introduce a false account of the work.

### P13 — The target or extent of the action unstated

- **Taxonomy:** N3 · Concrete actions and actual actors
- **Group:** action
- **Ask:** “Acts on what, exactly? Separate from what? Read or write? All of them or some?”
- **Problem when:** The operation is understandable, but what it applies to, what it excludes, or how far it reaches is left open and matters to the reader.
- **Not a problem when:** The target is established, or the extent is deliberately left to the reader's judgment and the text says so.
- **Distinguish from:** P11 when the operation itself is unclear; P18 when the missing boundary belongs to a rule or recommendation rather than an action.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A handover note about a project folder that holds exported PDFs and a notes subfolder. The reader will act on it without asking.
  - **Bad:** “Clean up the project folder, but leave the notes alone.”
  - **Good:** “Delete the exported PDFs in the project folder. Do not delete or move the notes subfolder.”
  - **What changes:** Two gaps are addressed: “clean up” now names the operation and its target, and “leave alone” states which operations it excludes. Naming the folder and the notes did not say what happens to each.

### P14 — An instruction whose addressee is unclear across delivery surfaces

- **Taxonomy:** N3 · Concrete actions and actual actors; also N6 · Connected sections and compatible claims
- **Group:** action
- **Ask:** “Who is being told to do this, and can the audience tell it is not them?”
- **Problem when:** Material read or shown to one audience contains an action meant for a different audience, such as a presenter's step inside a read-aloud script or an operator's step inside a customer-facing line, with nothing marking the change of addressee.
- **Not a problem when:** The surface marks the note as private, or the audience action is intended. A narrated demonstration step inside read-aloud speech, such as “Next, open Settings” while the presenter does so on screen, is not addressee-ambiguous by itself; it matches only when the sentence asks for something the audience would take as its own action and could not or should not perform, such as pointing out or confirming a control. Whether the audience is expected to follow along on its own devices is one document-level question: if the script never answers it, record it once under P26 or P31, anchored to the first step that depends on it, not under P14 at every step.
- **Distinguish from:** P12 when there is one audience and the actor is simply unnamed; P29 when the surfaces address different subjects rather than different addressees; P26 or P31 for whether the audience should follow along at all.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A customer-support call script. The agent reads the spoken lines aloud, and the customer hears them. Some lines are actions for the agent.
  - **Bad:** Spoken line: “Now click Reset password and wait for the email.”
  - **Good:** Agent note: “Click Reset password on the account page.” Spoken line: “I have sent you a reset link. Please open the email and follow it.”
  - **What changes:** The agent's action moves to a private note and the spoken line addresses the customer. As written, the customer would look for a button they do not have.

### P15 — Other unclear action or actor

- **Taxonomy:** N3 · Concrete actions and actual actors
- **Group:** action
- **Use when:** A supported gap matches N3's question but none of P10–P14. State the action and the missing part in the label.

### P16 — A judgment word or step without the purpose or criterion that gives it meaning

- **Taxonomy:** N4 · Purpose, scope and judgment criteria
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Right for what? Good by what standard? What is this step for?”
- **Problem when:** Terms such as right, good, useful, better, significant, appropriate, or a step presented without its purpose, depend on a standard that is neither stated nor reasonably established, and different standards change what counts as success.
- **Not a problem when:** The purpose or criterion is established nearby or is conventional for this recipient. A qualitative criterion is sufficient; do not demand a numerical one.
- **Distinguish from:** P17 when the word is about sufficiency or readiness; P19 when the missing element is a comparison baseline; P11 when the operation itself, not its standard, is unclear.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** The framework is meant to help select cases that exercise the moderation decisions being evaluated; this question is not about whether recorded answers are correct.
  - **Bad:** “Does the framework help us find the right examples?”
  - **Good:** “Does the framework help us find cases that exercise the moderation decisions we want to evaluate?”
  - **What changes:** “Right” has an explicit purpose. Relevant test situations and correctly answered examples are different meanings; the critic should establish which the author intends.

### P17 — Sufficiency or readiness declared without the use it must serve

- **Taxonomy:** N4 · Purpose, scope and judgment criteria
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Enough for what? Ready for what, and what remains unchecked?”
- **Problem when:** Enough, complete, ready, done or similar is stated or requested without the task the state must support, so the reader cannot tell what would satisfy it or what it does not yet cover.
- **Not a problem when:** The task is established, or the outcome is genuinely unresolved and the text says so.
- **Distinguish from:** P16 for other judgment words; P30 when two places define the state differently.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** Reviewers need a comment's surrounding post and applicable rules to judge an AI-generated reply.
  - **Bad:** “Can we obtain enough context to make each example usable?”
  - **Good:** “For each comment selected for the model evaluation, can we obtain its parent post and the applicable rules so reviewers can judge the AI's reply?”
  - **What changes:** The information and its use explain what “enough” means. No arbitrary quantity of context is required; sufficiency follows from the judgment the reviewer must make.
- **Example, the readiness form:**
  - **Situation:** Installation and required connection checks have passed, but nobody has yet tried the intended task.
  - **Bad:** “The setup is ready.”
  - **Good:** “The setup is ready for the first trial: the app is installed and the required connections are available. Whether it produces a usable result still needs checking.”
  - **What changes:** Readiness for a trial is distinguished from successful use. Ask “ready for what?” Preserve the unresolved outcome rather than inventing a success threshold or implying it has been met.

### P18 — The scope or boundary of a rule, recommendation or exemption unstated

- **Taxonomy:** N4 · Purpose, scope and judgment criteria
- **Group:** action
- **Ask:** “Which cases does this cover? Exempt from what, exactly? Separate from what?”
- **Problem when:** A rule, default, recommendation or exemption is stated with a qualifier such as trusted work, normal use, most cases, external items, but the boundary that decides whether a given case falls inside it is neither stated nor reasonably established.
- **Not a problem when:** The boundary is established elsewhere in the delivery, is conventional for the recipient, or is intentionally left to judgment and the text says so.
- **Distinguish from:** P13 when the missing boundary belongs to a specific action rather than a rule; P16 when the qualifier is a judgment word without a criterion.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A policy update in a team channel read by staff and contractors. One review step is being waived for some work.
  - **Bad:** “Client work is exempt from the review step.”
  - **Good:** “Work delivered directly to a client is exempt from the internal style review. It still needs the accuracy check. Internal documents keep both steps.”
  - **What changes:** The exemption now has a boundary, which work, and a scope, which step. Without them a reader can skip a check that still applies, or apply one that does not.

### P19 — A comparison or difference without its reference point

- **Taxonomy:** N4 · Purpose, scope and judgment criteria
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Faster than what? Higher than what? Differs from what?”
- **Problem when:** A comparative, a change or a difference is stated without the baseline it is measured against, and the reader needs the baseline to understand or act on the claim.
- **Not a problem when:** The baseline is the obvious previous state or the only alternative in play.
- **Distinguish from:** P16 when the word is an absolute judgment rather than a comparison; P30 when two stated values conflict.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A release note read by people on several plans.
  - **Bad:** “Uploads are now faster and the limit is higher.”
  - **Good:** “On the Team plan, uploads now take less time than in the previous release, and the file-size limit is higher than before.”
  - **What changes:** Each comparison names its baseline and its scope. No figure is required; the reader only needs to know what is being compared with what.

### P20 — The condition or threshold for a decision or escalation unstated

- **Taxonomy:** N4 · Purpose, scope and judgment criteria
- **Group:** action
- **Ask:** “How would the reader know the condition has been met? After how many tries, or on which sign?”
- **Problem when:** An instruction turns on a condition such as if still blocked, when critical, if something differs, and the reader cannot tell what state or sign satisfies it.
- **Not a problem when:** The sign is unambiguous in context, or the judgment is intentionally left to the reader and the text says so. A qualitative test is sufficient.
- **Distinguish from:** P10 when the condition is clear and the action for it is missing; P16 when the condition is a judgment word without a criterion.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** An on-call runbook for a nightly data job. Retrying is cheap; contacting the vendor is costly.
  - **Bad:** “Retry the job. If it is still failing, contact the vendor.”
  - **Good:** “Retry the job once. If the second run fails with the same error, contact the vendor. If it fails with a different error, open an internal ticket instead.”
  - **What changes:** “Still failing” gains a test: how many retries and which failure counts. The threshold stays qualitative; what matters is that the reader can tell when it is met.

### P21 — Other missing purpose, scope or criterion

- **Taxonomy:** N4 · Purpose, scope and judgment criteria
- **Group:** meaning
- **Use when:** A supported gap matches N4's question but none of P16–P20. State the judgment and the missing basis in the label.

### P22 — An example or scenario begins before its situation is introduced

- **Taxonomy:** N5 · Prerequisite context available to the reader
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Does the recipient know what this example is about and why its details matter?”
- **Problem when:** Details of an example are used, compared or checked before the reader is told the situation they belong to.
- **Not a problem when:** The situation is introduced in the same passage or on a visible surface at that moment.
- **Distinguish from:** P04 when a single name, not a situation, is unintroduced; P25 when the missing item is a reason or prior event rather than an example's setting.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A workshop demonstrates rewriting fictional notes about a page waiting for two screenshots before publication. Participants have not been introduced to the scenario.
  - **Bad:** “Open the notes beside the draft. Has it kept the missing screenshots?”
  - **Good:** “In this fictional example, a page is waiting for two screenshots before publication. We are turning the project notes into an update for colleagues. Check whether the draft preserves that dependency.”
  - **What changes:** Participants can understand what the details concern and why they matter. Naming the source file alone would not introduce the situation. An adequately introduced visible example could also supply this context.

### P23 — The explanation arrives after the point that depends on it

- **Taxonomy:** N5 · Prerequisite context available to the reader
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Where is this defined, and is that before or after the reader needs it?”
- **Problem when:** A definition, distinction or explanation exists in the delivery but comes later than the instruction or claim that relies on it.
- **Not a problem when:** The audience already has the knowledge, or the order is intentional and the text signals that the explanation follows.
- **Distinguish from:** P04 when no introduction exists anywhere; P24 when the explanation exists but only privately.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** Beginners hear about tools required by Skills before the presentation explains what a Skill is.
  - **Bad:** “First, check that the tools required by your Skills are available.” Several sections later: “A Skill is a reusable set of instructions for a particular kind of work.”
  - **Good:** “A Skill is a reusable set of instructions for a particular kind of work. Some Skills use tools to carry out those instructions. First, check that the tools required by your Skills are available.”
  - **What changes:** The prerequisite is supplied before the instruction depends on it. A definition somewhere in the document is insufficient here; established audience knowledge or intentional withholding can warrant a different order.

### P24 — The explanation exists only on a private surface

- **Taxonomy:** N5 · Prerequisite context available to the reader
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Does this explanation reach the audience, or only the author or facilitator?”
- **Problem when:** The understanding needed is in the author's notes, the facilitator's instructions, an uninspected link or private working context, and the audience-facing material assumes it.
- **Not a problem when:** A visible surface supplies it, or the audience legitimately has it already. Do not treat an uninspected link as proof either way.
- **Distinguish from:** P14 when a private instruction leaks into the audience material, the reverse direction; P23 when the explanation is public but late.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** Participants must compare models tested on the same workflow. Each table row is one evaluation run, but only the facilitator's private notes explain that unit.
  - **Bad:** Private note: “Each row is one evaluation run.” Spoken explanation: “Compare these rows.”
  - **Good:** Spoken explanation: “Each row records one evaluation run of a model on a workflow. Compare the rows for the same workflow.”
  - **What changes:** The explanation reaches the audience instead of remaining in the author's working context. A visible table caption could supply it instead; it need not be repeated aloud if already clear. Uninspected material does not establish that the audience has or lacks this explanation.

### P25 — A reason or prior event assumed but never given

- **Taxonomy:** N5 · Prerequisite context available to the reader
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Why is this so? What happened before? Does this reader know?”
- **Problem when:** The point relies on a decision, event, reason or distinction the intended reader has not received, often signalled by phrases such as given last week's decision, as agreed, for the usual reason.
- **Not a problem when:** The reader was part of the earlier exchange, or the reason is deliberately withheld and the text says so.
- **Distinguish from:** P04 when the gap is an unknown name; P22 when the gap is an example's setting.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A project update sent to a stakeholder who was not in this week's meeting.
  - **Bad:** “Given Tuesday's decision, we are pausing the pilot until the vendor responds.”
  - **Good:** “On Tuesday the steering group decided not to extend the pilot contract on the current terms. We are pausing the pilot until the vendor responds to our revised terms.”
  - **What changes:** The prior event is supplied to the reader who needs it. In a thread where Tuesday's decision was already discussed, the original is sufficient.

### P26 — Other missing prerequisite

- **Taxonomy:** N5 · Prerequisite context available to the reader
- **Group:** meaning
- **Use when:** A supported gap matches N5's question but none of P22–P25. State what the reader needs and where it is missing.

### P27 — Parallel points that silently answer different questions

- **Taxonomy:** N6 · Connected sections and compatible claims
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “What question does each of these points answer? Are they really parallel?”
- **Problem when:** Items presented as parallel evidence or parallel answers address different questions, so the reader treats an unrelated fact as support.
- **Not a problem when:** The points are parallel, or their different roles are stated.
- **Distinguish from:** P30 when the points conflict rather than diverge; P29 when the issue is a missing transition between topics.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A trial used the framework on 30 comment records. Its questions were clear and identified the source posts and rules needed; whether all those sources can be obtained is still unchecked.
  - **Bad:** “How useful is the framework? Its questions are clear. We have 30 comment records.”
  - **Good:** “We tried the framework on 30 comment records. Its questions were clear and helped us identify the source posts and rules each case needed. We have not yet checked whether those sources can be obtained for every record.”
  - **What changes:** The records' role in answering the section's question becomes explicit. A record count is not itself an assessment of the method. Identify each point's role before treating points as parallel evidence.

### P28 — Connected or sequential actions presented as alternatives

- **Taxonomy:** N6 · Connected sections and compatible claims
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Is this really either-or, or both in order?”
- **Problem when:** A contrast such as this, not that, or one, not many, implies a choice between actions that are actually connected or sequential.
- **Not a problem when:** The alternatives are genuine and unresolved, and the text preserves that.
- **Distinguish from:** P31 when the dependency is simply never stated rather than misrepresented; P10 when an action is missing altogether.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** Workflow status definitions need agreement, and existing table records will then need checking against those definitions.
  - **Bad:** “The fix is one document, not 147 edits.”
  - **Good:** “First, agree what each status in the Workflows table means. Then check the existing records against those definitions and update any that are inaccurate.”
  - **What changes:** Defining the statuses and correcting the records are connected steps. The original contrast could make the reader think the records would never need updating. State the actual relationship rather than imply a false choice.

### P29 — A change of topic, level or surface without a connection

- **Taxonomy:** N6 · Connected sections and compatible claims
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Why are we here now? What should the reader take from the switch?”
- **Problem when:** The subject, level of reasoning or delivery surface changes and nothing explains the move, including speech and visible material addressing different subjects, or screen order and speech order that do not match.
- **Not a problem when:** The structure is intentional and marked, or the shift is obvious from the delivery.
- **Distinguish from:** P14 when the surfaces differ in addressee rather than subject; P25 when the missing element is a background reason rather than a transition.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A presentation moves from setup checks to handling usage limits.
  - **Bad:** Screen: “Usage & billing.” Spoken explanation: “Check your model and access settings.”
  - **Good:** Screen: “Usage & billing.” Spoken explanation: “We have finished checking setup. This screen shows usage limits and any available resets; use it when usage limits interrupt your work.”
  - **What changes:** The speech explains why the screen changed and what the audience should understand from it. Clear screen directions and clear sentences can still form a disconnected explanation when assessed together.

### P30 — Claims in different places that do not fit together

- **Taxonomy:** N6 · Connected sections and compatible claims
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “Read together, are these compatible? Which one governs?”
- **Problem when:** Two statements define, describe or require the same thing differently, unlike claims are presented as equivalent, or one statement overreaches what another allows, and the text leaves the reader to reconcile them.
- **Not a problem when:** The statements describe different viewpoints, times or levels and their relationship is clear.
- **Distinguish from:** P27 when the points differ in question rather than conflict; P17 and P19 when the problem sits within one statement.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A setup guide. Its checklist says setup is complete when three checks pass; a later section says a fourth check must pass before first use.
  - **Bad:** Section 2: “Setup is complete when the three checks pass.” Section 5: “Do not start until the connection check passes.”
  - **Good:** Section 2: “Setup is complete when the three installation checks pass. Before first use, the connection check in Section 5 must also pass.”
  - **What changes:** The two statements are reconciled at the point where the reader would otherwise conclude they are done.

### P31 — A dependency or connection the account needs but no sentence states

- **Taxonomy:** N6 · Connected sections and compatible claims
- **Group:** meaning
- **Ask:** “What must the reader already have understood, or have done, for this part to work, and does any sentence say so?”
- **Problem when:** The parts are individually clear, but the account depends on a link between them, such as an order of stages or a shared object, that appears nowhere.
- **Not a problem when:** The link is obvious from the delivery or conventional for the recipient.
- **Distinguish from:** P28 when the link is misrepresented as a choice; P08 when the missing link is between two objects within one passage; P20 when a stated condition lacks its test.
- **Example:**
  - **Situation:** A how-to describes exporting a report in one section and sharing it in another. Sharing only works after the export has finished, and nothing says so.
  - **Bad:** Section A: “Export the report from the dashboard.” Section B: “Share the report link with the client.”
  - **Good:** Section B: “Once the export in Section A has finished and the report appears in your Files, share its link with the client.”
  - **What changes:** The dependency is stated where the second action begins. Each section was clear on its own.

### P32 — Other disconnected or incompatible account

- **Taxonomy:** N6 · Connected sections and compatible claims
- **Group:** meaning
- **Use when:** A supported gap matches N6's question but none of P27–P31. State the parts and the missing connection in the label.
