


# Recipient and purpose

Shared checks for the kind of answer, detail and presentation the actual exchange needs. A recipient’s title alone does not determine these requirements.

Apply these checks through [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md); its application rules govern every item.

## P1. The communication serves the intended purpose and aspect

- **What to examine:** The kind of answer and emphasis requested for this exchange: explanation, exploration, recommendation, progress report, instruction or another purpose.
- **What counts as a problem:** The communication answers a different kind of question or adopts an inappropriate perspective, even though its individual statements are clear.
- **Why it matters:** The recipient must translate the message into the answer they needed or may respond to the wrong task.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Use recipient, purpose, medium and the local role of a passage together. The same manager may need technical or operational communication. Mixed purposes are legitimate when connected. Do not turn an explanation of work into instructions to carry it out.

## P2. Detail supports the reader’s present task

- **What to examine:** Whether each material detail helps the recipient identify the subject, follow the reasoning or make the judgment needed now.
- **What counts as a problem:** Necessary technical or contextual meaning has been removed, or irrelevant detail materially obscures the answer and burdens the reader.
- **Why it matters:** The message becomes either too vague to use or unnecessarily difficult to absorb.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Technical names, quantities and mechanisms can matter to any recipient. Use the depth required by the task; do not ban mechanics for managers or equate shorter with clearer. Supporting detail may be separate when the main answer remains sufficient.

## P3. Wording and layout reduce reading effort

- **What to examine:** Sentence construction, vocabulary, repetition and the organisation of parallel points, qualifications and reasoning.
- **What counts as a problem:** The presentation makes supported meaning unnecessarily hard to follow, hides a useful list or buries a necessary relationship or qualification.
- **Why it matters:** The reader spends avoidable effort unpacking the text and may miss an important distinction.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Choose prose, bullets, tables, headings and labelled fields by their function and the requested medium. A sentence may contain closely related clauses; multiple ifs or brackets are not automatic defects. Identify the actual difficulty rather than treating stylistic preference as error.

## P4. Voice fits the author, recipient and medium

- **What to examine:** The author’s requested voice and the language appropriate to this recipient, relationship, purpose and medium.
- **What counts as a problem:** Wording violates an applicable voice instruction, misrepresents the relationship, or creates an unintended demand, distance or attitude that interferes with the exchange.
- **Why it matters:** The message may represent the author poorly or make the recipient misunderstand how they are being approached.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** First person, warmth and formality are contextual choices. Headings and labelled fields are legitimate when useful or requested. Apply personal phrase and meeting preferences only within their stated scope. Evaluate invented offers or commitments for fidelity too; one finding may cover both concerns.

## P5. Supporting references are usable in the intended delivery

- **What to examine:** The identity, purpose and expected accessibility of supporting documents, links or passages necessary for the recipient’s use of the communication.
- **What counts as a problem:** An essential reference is unidentifiable, points to the wrong material, is known to be unavailable to that reader, or substitutes for the substantive answer the message was meant to provide.
- **Why it matters:** The reader cannot inspect the needed support or must reconstruct the answer from inaccessible or excessive material.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Do not require a link for every noun or an evidence appendix for every message. A source-review task may legitimately require reading sources. The critic’s own access does not prove the recipient’s access; state an unverified access limit rather than claiming confirmed failure.
