
# Meaning and fidelity

Shared checks for required substance, faithful claims and preserved intent. Apply to both draft criticism and the meaning that proposed mitigations would introduce.

Apply these checks through [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md); its application rules govern every item.

## M1. The requested answer is present

- **What to examine:** Whether the communication supplies the answer, comparison, explanation or other substance required by the actual request and intended use.
- **What counts as a problem:** A necessary part of that meaning is absent or replaced by related information, without an adequate explanation of why it cannot yet be supplied.
- **Why it matters:** The recipient may understand every sentence while still lacking the answer they needed from the exchange.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Use the request and context, not only topics present in the draft. Do not require every conceivable answer. A justified limitation can be appropriate; an intentionally open choice needs no recommendation. If the requirement is unknown, qualify the finding.

## M2. Claims remain faithful to available evidence and intent

- **What to examine:** Factual claims and wording attributed to the author, including facts, motives, effort, offers, commitments and added implications.
- **What counts as a problem:** The text invents or materially changes those meanings, treats old illustrative wording as current fact, or copies editorial instructions into recipient content.
- **Why it matters:** A readable message can misrepresent the situation or commit its author to something they did not mean.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Allow faithful paraphrase and development authorised by the brief. Commissioned fiction or understood hypotheticals are not false factual claims. Missing evidence is not proof of falsity: identify the specific uncertainty instead of supplying a plausible fact.

## M3. Status and approval retain their actual scope

- **What to examine:** The stated maturity, completion, selection and approval of each material claim, action or artifact, compared with the available evidence.
- **What counts as a problem:** Exploration becomes a decision, a proposal becomes implementation, partial feedback becomes broader approval, or the wording otherwise misrepresents an established state in a way that changes the reader’s understanding.
- **Why it matters:** The reader may act as though work is ready, authorised or settled when it is not.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Judge the whole statement and actual scope; words such as is or will do not determine status alone. A draft can contain settled facts alongside open choices. Do not invent an approval requirement or force repeated status labels where the meaning is clear.

## M4. Intended openness stays open

- **What to examine:** Which choices, concepts, estimates or uncertainties the current communication is meant to leave open, and what any proposed mitigation would make more definite.
- **What counts as a problem:** The draft or critique proposal adds unsupported finality, or the critic treats appropriate openness as a required missing answer.
- **Why it matters:** The review can change exploration into commitment and misrepresent the author’s intent while appearing to improve clarity.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Openness is specific, not immunity from review. Known facts and unclear subjects remain checkable. A vague authoring brief may permit a concrete proposal. Establish intended openness from the task; if uncertain, qualify the judgment. Do not force uncertainty labels into every sentence.

## M5. Reasoning, source attribution and conclusions stay connected

- **What to examine:** How source statements, observations and interpretations support the actual conclusion, recommendation or explanation; whether consequential attribution is accurate.
- **What counts as a problem:** A claim exceeds its basis, the needed inferential connection is missing, interpretation is presented as a source’s words, or a result is treated as proof of a method without explaining why.
- **Why it matters:** The reader may accept a stronger conclusion than the evidence warrants or mistake the author’s reading for someone else’s position.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Match support to the claim’s strength and the reader’s purpose. Ordinary statements need no exhaustive proof trail. A missing explanation does not establish that the author never reasoned about it. Propose clarifying, substantiating or qualifying the claim without inventing evidence or doing the underlying task.

## M6. Applicable corrections and exclusions survive

- **What to examine:** The current target text against applicable corrections, removals, factual updates, requested wording and standing author preferences.
- **What counts as a problem:** A revision restores removed content, ignores a still-applicable correction, preserves a known superseded claim, or applies an instruction to the wrong text or scope.
- **Why it matters:** The message can become clearer while becoming less faithful to what the author actually asked to communicate.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Respect the scope and authority of the instruction; a removal from one message is not a project-wide ban. Later explicit direction may change an earlier edit. Recency alone does not settle conflicting sources, and unavailable editing history limits the claim that a correction was lost.

## M7. Necessary meaning survives shortening and revision

- **What to examine:** The required meanings carried by the current text after shortening, restructuring or other revision, compared with the brief and relevant earlier version when available.
- **What counts as a problem:** An edit removes a necessary referent, relationship, answer or qualification, or the only clarification remains in a private editing exchange rather than the intended delivery.
- **Why it matters:** Brevity transfers missing explanation back to the reader and can change the substance of the message.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Remove optional points and repetition while preserving required meaning. Pronouns and shorter phrases are acceptable when clear. Judge the actual delivery context, not a forced standalone standard. Respect explicit length limits without inventing a universal target length.
