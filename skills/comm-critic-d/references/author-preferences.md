
# Author preferences

Apply when reviewing outgoing communication written in the author's name: the person on whose behalf the communication is prepared. These are that author's preferences for representing them, including in their workplace communication; they are not organisation-wide policies or rules for other authors. Current explicit instructions may override a preference for a particular text.

Apply these checks through [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md); its application rules govern every item. These checks supplement the shared guides.

## E1. No unsolicited call or meeting offers

- **What to examine:** Offers or suggestions to hold, arrange or join a call or meeting in the author's name.
- **What counts as a problem:** The draft introduces such an offer without the author requesting or authorising it, or restores a meeting line they asked to remove.
- **Why it matters:** It creates an unwanted invitation or commitment and misrepresents how the author wants to communicate.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Applies to outgoing communication written for the author. Reporting an existing meeting or discussing one that the task explicitly concerns is not an unsolicited offer. Do not extend this preference to other authors.

## E2. Personal messages use a direct, natural voice

- **What to examine:** First-person expression of the author's own actions and views, conversational phrasing and the presentation of personal messages to colleagues.
- **What counts as a problem:** The message reads as a field-value form with labels such as “Next step:”, “Status:” or “For your approval:”; uses procedural softeners such as “please find” or “as per”; or adds expressive filler and invented motivations instead of the work-related point.
- **Why it matters:** The wording represents the author in a voice they rejected and can obscure the substance or relationship behind the message.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Use a warm, direct register for personal work messages. First person does not require every sentence to begin with “I”. Bullets for parallel points remain useful. Requested templates, tables and labelled fields in structured documents are legitimate; do not apply this preference to the critique report itself.

## E3. Requests to the author's manager respect the intended working relationship

- **What to examine:** How a message from the author addresses their manager, especially requests and statements about decision responsibility.
- **What counts as a problem:** The draft uses “What I need from you” as a demand or “my recommendation is that it's you” to assign the manager the final say, or introduces an equivalent presumptive framing unsupported by the brief.
- **Why it matters:** It can make a request sound like an instruction to the author's manager or imply authority and responsibilities that the communication has not established.
- **When it applies—and its limits:** Applies to this author–manager relationship when it is relevant to the task. Keep requests explicit and preserve established responsibilities; respectful wording must not make the ask vague or create an extra approval requirement. Do not infer a universal ban on direct requests from this preference.
